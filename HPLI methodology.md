# HPLI — Methodology

This document records the methodological decisions behind the HPLI implementation in this repository: what each rule is, why it was chosen, and where it comes from. The code changes as the scripts are extended; the reasoning recorded here should not need rewriting each time. Where the implementation departs from the values published in [Vandevoorde et al. (2025)](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1748-9326/ae269b), that is stated explicitly in the section concerned.

This documentation is still being structured and expanded.

**Two sources are cited throughout.** [Vandevoorde et al. (2025)](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1748-9326/ae269b) (*Environmental Research Letters*) is the published description of the indicator, and the source of *Table 1*'s normalisation thresholds and aggregation weights. And the doctoral dissertation it comes from, [Vandevoorde (2025)](https://hdl.handle.net/2078.5/264116), *Three tools for the reduction of pesticide impacts* (UCLouvain), whose appendices D to I carry the detail the article had no room for: the derivation of the thresholds, the treatment of missing and unbounded data, the persistence coefficient and the weighting. Where this document says “the dissertation”, it means those appendices.

## Purpose and scope

`HPLI score.R` computes the HPLI-EU score (the 20-metric version of the Harmonised Pesticide Load Indicator) for every active substance of a PPDB/BPDB export, following [Vandevoorde et al. (2025)](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1748-9326/ae269b).

**This is a load score, not a risk score.** The HPLI computed here is the *intrinsic* hazard of one kg of active substance, independent of how much of it is applied: it accounts for neither application rate, nor frequency, nor treated area. Comparing two substances' HPLI values says which is more hazardous per kg, and nothing about which contributes more hazard in practice on a field or across a territory (a low-load substance applied at a high dose easily outweighs a high-load substance applied sparingly). A meaningful comparison between substances, or between products, requires multiplying the load score by a use-quantity term: the annual applied quantity (for the risk score) or a product's standard dose (for tools such as the Pesti-Score). Neither is computed here; both depend on use data this implementation does not read.

The scope is deliberately limited to HPLI-EU. Specific local versions (such as the 27-metric Walloon HPLI-Wal) are not yet implemented here and are left to later iterations, once the HPLI-EU implementation is a settled and transferable reference (section [Planned extensions](#planned-extensions)). Weight calculation from PPDB updates is implemented (section [Weight calculation](#weight-calculation)).

**Implementation layout.** The code is divided into several scripts, split along the line that separates what a different HPLI version would change from what it would not. `HPLI parameters.R` holds the indicator's own definition (metrics, thresholds, compartments, default weights). `HPLI import.R` reads and completes the metrics from a PPDB export. `HPLI weights.R` calculates the weights from a PPDB export. `HPLI score.R` holds the settings, the scoring, the persistence coefficient and the aggregation. `HPLI visualisation.R` draws the rose diagrams (section [Rose plot visualisation](#rose-plot-visualisation)) from a saved results workbook (section [PPDB licensing and what may be shared](#ppdb-licensing-and-what-may-be-shared)).

## Data source and thresholds

Hazard data is read from an AERU PPDB export laid out as four files (`General.xlsx`, `Fate.xlsx`, `Ecotox.xlsx`, `Human.xlsx`), reshaped into five internal tables: identification, fate, terrestrial and aquatic ecotoxicity, human toxicity. The values published in [Vandevoorde et al. (2025)](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1748-9326/ae269b), and every figure quoted in this document, come from the PPDB export of **3 May 2024**. The PPDB is revised continuously (properties and quality flags alike, not only new substances) so a more recent export will not reproduce these figures exactly. `HPLI import.R` documents what to change if a PPDB header is modified in the future.

Every sheet is read with all columns as text. Were the columns typed automatically instead, any cell a spreadsheet reader cannot parse as a number — such as a range (`"10-20"`), a comparator (`"<LOD"`, `">1000"`) or a placeholder (`"nc"`, `"Not calculated"`) — would arrive as a missing value, before any of the following data treatment could see what it actually said. An imprecise or bounded observation would then be indistinguishable from an absent one, and would collect the worst-case substitution of section [Missing-data and range-value policy](#missing-data-and-range-value-policy) instead of being read for what it states. Reading everything as text is what allows sections [Missing-data and range-value policy](#missing-data-and-range-value-policy) and [Data quality traceability](#data-quality-traceability) to tell those cases apart.

### Normalisation thresholds

The normalisation thresholds and aggregation weights follow *Table 1* of [Vandevoorde et al. (2025)](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1748-9326/ae269b), encoded as one row per threshold level and one column per metric, following the published table's own layout so that the two can be checked against each other directly, metric by metric, rather than through a differently shaped encoding. Their derivation is set out in the dissertation's appendix.

### Extrapolation beyond the thresholds

Extrapolation past the outermost thresholds is intentionally asymmetric.

For the twelve metrics whose score *decreases* with the raw value, the 1.50 threshold already sits at a raw value of 0: a null LD50, LC50, EC50, NOEC or KFOC. Nothing lies beyond it, 0 being the physical minimum of a concentration or a dose, so 1.50 is their attainable maximum (the score of an absolutely hazardous substance rather than of a merely extreme one). In contrast, the 0 threshold floors all scores that would otherwise extrapolate a negative value: diquat's KFOC of roughly 1.08 × 10⁶ ml/g, far beyond the null-hazard threshold of 10 000 ml/g, extrapolates to about −30 but is floored at 0.

For the four metrics whose score *increases* with the raw value (soil DT50, water DT50, GUS and BCF), a raw value beyond the last threshold extrapolates along that segment's slope, without a ceiling, potentially leading to a value **above the “very high load” level**. Diquat's soil DT50 of 2345 days accordingly scores 2.6 rather than being capped at 1.50: an extreme outlier should read as extreme. As stated above this does not apply for the 0 threshold: only the high-hazard side is left open.

## Missing-data and range-value policy

### Missing data

Two policies are available:

- Under `"hpli_precautionary"` (the default), substances below 60% data coverage across the 20 metrics are excluded, matching the threshold adopted in the paper. Any missing value is substituted with the metric's high-hazard threshold, that is, the raw value at which the metric's score reaches 1.00. This follows the precautionary substitution of [Vandevoorde et al. (2025)](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1748-9326/ae269b) (section 3.2): missing values take the *high*-hazard threshold (not the more extreme 1.50 “very high load” level).
- Under `"complete_only"`, a substance is scored only when all metrics have data.

The same 60% bar has a second, quite different use, upstream of anything the current code implementation does: it is also what selects **the metrics themselves**. A metric enters the indicator when at least 60% of the substances in scope carry data for it. The two uses are easy to conflate (one filters rows, the other decides columns) and the second is what makes the metric list depend on which substances the indicator is meant to cover. Section [Adapting the indicator to another metric set](#adapting-the-indicator-to-another-metric-set) takes this up.

The two bars are set to the same 60% deliberately, for consistency, but they answer different questions and there is no reason they must agree: one could reasonably demand better coverage of a metric before admitting it to the indicator than of a substance before scoring it, or the reverse. They should therefore be two independent settings rather than one shared constant. Only the substance-side bar exists in this implementation code today, the metric selection being an input rather than a computation (section [Adapting the indicator to another metric set](#adapting-the-indicator-to-another-metric-set)); introducing the metric-side bar as its own setting will come with future update of the code (the first item of section [Planned extensions](#planned-extensions)).

Another alternative (imputing the mean observed within the substance's pesticide functional group, as the UK-PLI does; [Tzilivakis et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1080/03601234.2025.2610127)) is not yet implemented in these scripts, and will also come with future updates (section [Planned extensions](#planned-extensions)). The current substitution is a deliberately conservative default, not a claim that it is the most informative one.

### Ranges

A cell holding two numbers and no other qualifier, such as `"10-20"`, states an *imprecise* value, not an *unknown* one, and the two should not be conflated: a bounded value still carries information that a missing one does not. Such a cell is therefore resolved rather than discarded: under `"worst_case"` (the default), it is resolved to whichever end is more hazardous for that metric, under `"mean"` to the average of the two ends. Which end counts as worse is derived from the thresholds themselves (rather than from a second hardcoded list), so it cannot drift out of step with the definition of “worse” used everywhere else. Comparator prefixes (`"<LOD"`, `">1000"`) are unaffected: the number after the comparator is read as before, and the comparator is recorded separately (section [Data quality traceability](#data-quality-traceability)).

Ranges are rare in practice. Scanning the full 3 May 2024 export (2711 substances across the 20 metrics used here) turned up exactly one: butafenacil's water DT50, recorded as the free text `"Slow, DT50 25-30 days"`. Water DT50's score increases with the raw value, so `"worst_case"` resolves it to 30. That this happens to equal what the precautionary substitution would have supplied is a coincidence of this one row; the substance's data-coverage count and its list of missing metrics are still correctly affected, since the value is no longer treated as absent.

## Completing soil DT50, KFOC, BCF and GUS before normalisation

Four fate metrics are completed from secondary PPDB fields before scoring:

- **Soil DT50** uses the Field value where available; otherwise, between Lab and Typical, whichever carries the higher PPDB quality band, the two being averaged on a tie; otherwise whichever of the two exists.
- **KFOC** falls back to KOC when KFOC itself is missing.
- **BCF** falls back to a QSAR-type regression from the completed KFOC when the measured value is missing.
- **GUS** is always computed from the completed soil DT50 and KFOC, never read from the export.

Anything still unavailable after this is left missing, exactly like any other absent metric, and picked up by the precautionary substitution of section [Missing-data and range-value policy](#missing-data-and-range-value-policy).

The quality-band comparison reads the PPDB's 1–5 band as a confidence score in which higher means more reliable, following the  's own description of it as based on perceived reliability.

**GUS is always recomputed.** Reading it from a GUS column where one exists and computing it elsewhere would make the metric internally inconsistent across the dataset (two substances' GUS scores would no longer be derived the same way, which matters more for a metric that also feeds a correlation-based weight than the marginal gain of using a pre-computed value). The published HPLI recomputes it for every substance. Its provenance in the traceability output (section [Data quality traceability](#data-quality-traceability)) is therefore always `"completed"`: here GUS is a derived quantity, never a measurement.

**PPDB placeholders in categorical fields read as missing, not as “no”.** The database writes “No data”, “not available”, “nd”, “nc”, “not calculated” and “not determined” in a categorical hazard field that was not assessed. They are treated as missing, and so receive the precautionary substitution of section [Missing-data and range-value policy](#missing-data-and-range-value-policy).

### Chemically inert substances

By default, every substance's missing fate metrics fall through to the same precautionary substitution as any other missing metric: the precautionary principle applied uniformly rather than with an exception carved out for them. That default is deliberately conservative, and it overstates the environmental-fate hazard of substances that are chemically inert with no plausible leaching mechanism at all: quartz sand, kaolin, silica, kieselgur, gypsum, whose only documented chronic hazard is inhalation-related, such as silicosis, rather than environmental accumulation.

**Detecting them automatically was tried and rejected**, and the reasons are worth recording so that the attempt is not repeated. The PPDB's `Substance origin` field is a production and regulatory classification, not a physicochemical one: kaolin and kaolin calcined are chemically identical yet labelled `Natural` and `Synthetic` respectively. A text pattern over the DT50 notes (“non-degradable”, “stable in soil”) fails on a concrete counter-example: barium polysulphide matches the same wording yet is a heavy-metal compound with documented chronic barium poisoning, and so belongs with the accumulation-relevant group rather than the inert one. No wording available in the database separates the two reliably.

The approach is therefore a **hand-verified list**, `HPLI score - natural inert substances.csv`: one row per substance, each checked individually against the PPDB's chronic-toxicity fields for a documented accumulation mechanism, with a comment recording why it was included or excluded (so that a future PPDB refresh does not require re-deriving the reasoning) and the PPDB ID, PPDB version and review date for traceability. Matching is on the PPDB ID, which is stable across versions where names are not necessarily. The file is optional: without it, every substance is treated precautionarily.

For a substance on that list, a still-missing environmental-fate metric is substituted with the **null**-hazard threshold rather than the high-hazard one, on the reading that the pathway the metric measures does not apply rather than that its value is unknown. Real data takes priority and is left untouched either way. Note that the null-hazard raw value is not 0 for every metric: it is 0 for soil and water DT50 and for BCF, but around 10 000 ml/g (well retained) for a *high* KFOC and around −18 (non-leaching) for low GUS.

**Whether the persistence coefficient should apply to these substances at all** is a related question, answered per pathway rather than in one sweep. The coefficient assumes that exposure duration tracks environmental persistence, which does not hold for a mechanism driven by exposure *frequency* (kaolin and quartz sand are flagged carcinogenic in the PPDB on the strength of repeated-inhalation silicosis, not of accumulation in the environment). For the substances on the list, $k_{human}$ is therefore forced to 0, that mechanism having been verified as not persistence-related for each of them. $k_{water}$ is deliberately *not* touched: two of them carry real, measured chronic aquatic NOEC values, and whether that exposure pathway is persistence-related has not been established either way (zeroing it without checking would silently discard real data). $k_{soil}$ has no independent use in the current scope of the indicator (as it only feeds the $k_{human}$ average), so the question is left for future development: it resurfaces as soon as a soil-based chronic ecotoxicity metric is taken into account, such as the earthworm chronic NOEC of the 27-metric version (section [Adapting the indicator to another metric set](#adapting-the-indicator-to-another-metric-set)), and the same evidence-first approach should apply then rather than the human-toxicity conclusion being assumed to carry over.

## Where the persistence coefficient is applied

The persistence coefficient

$$
k(DT_{50}, a) = \frac{1 - 2^{-a/DT_{50}}}{a \ln(2) / DT_{50}}
$$

is applied to every chronic-effect metric by multiplying its *normalised score* (*i.e.* after the metric has been mapped onto the 0–1.5 scale) rather than by adjusting the raw value before normalisation. This applies uniformly: to the two chronic aquatic ecotoxicity metrics (aquatic invertebrates NOEC, fish NOEC, using $k_{water}$ with $DT_{50}^{water}$) and to the four chronic human-toxicity metrics (carcinogenicity, cholinesterase inhibition, neurotoxicity, reprotoxicity, using $k_{human} = (k_{soil} + k_{water})/2$).

As the human-toxicity metrics are categorical in the PPDB and only become numeric once normalised (no/possible/yes → 0/0.5/1), the coefficient can only be applied post-normalisation for that compartment; applying it the same way everywhere avoids a compartment-dependent rule. More fundamentally, the coefficient exists to account for the decline of a substance's *effect* over time as it degrades, not for the intrinsic value of the toxicity endpoint itself. The two need not scale linearly with one another (which is precisely why normalisation is itself non-linear, piecewise between thresholds rather than a single line through the origin). Multiplying the already-normalised, unitless hazard score is coherent with adjusting the effect rather than the measurement.

**This is an assumed divergence from the published values, not a variant.** The implementation behind [Vandevoorde et al. (2025)](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1748-9326/ae269b) divides the raw value by $k$ *before* normalising, for the two numeric chronic aquatic metrics. The two approaches are not equivalent under a non-linear normalisation function, so the chronic aquatic invertebrate and fish NOEC scores computed here will not reproduce the published ones for any substance with a defined water DT50. On review, dividing the raw value is a logic error rather than an alternative convention: it rescales the *measurement* (a concentration in mg/l, whose relationship to hazard is the piecewise normalisation itself) as though degradation changed the toxicity endpoint, when what degradation attenuates is the exposure, and hence the effect. It also makes the same coefficient mean two different things within one indicator, since the categorical human-toxicity metrics have no raw value to divide and can only ever be adjusted post-normalisation. Applying $k$ uniformly to the normalised score is therefore treated here as the correct behaviour, and the published figures as superseded on this specific point. Anyone comparing this implementation against the published appendix should expect exactly this difference.

**Constants.** $a_{soil} = 180$ and $a_{water} = 7$, as published and inherited unchanged from the Danish PLI. An alternative pair derived from the threshold midpoints ($a_{soil} = 230$, $a_{water} = 33$) is proposed in the dissertation ([Vandevoorde (2025)](https://hdl.handle.net/2078.5/264116), appendix F.1); exposing the choice as a parameter is listed under section [Planned extensions](#planned-extensions).

**The coefficient assumes one application per season.** $k$ is the mean relative concentration over the window $a$ that follows a *single* application decaying at first order, so environmental persistence — proxied by DT50 — is the only thing setting how long exposure lasts. Chronic effects arising from repeated exposure through several applications in the same season are therefore not represented: a substance applied three times at short intervals scores as though it had been applied once. This is the same limitation section [Completing soil DT50, KFOC, BCF and GUS before normalisation](#completing-soil-dt50-kfoc-bcf-and-gus-before-normalisation) meets from another angle, where the chronic hazard of the inert substances is driven by exposure *frequency* rather than by persistence and $k_{human}$ is consequently forced to 0. Representing application frequency would require the use data this implementation does not read (section [Purpose and scope](#purpose-and-scope)).

**Missing DT50 in the coefficient itself.** $k(DT_{50}, a) \to 1$ as $DT_{50} \to \infty$: a long half-life means full chronic severity, no attenuation for degradation. So $k = 1$ reads as “assume persistent” (*i.e.* the precautionary direction). Soil and water DT50 are themselves two of the scored metrics, so under the precautionary policy a missing one is already substituted with its own high-hazard threshold (180 days for soil, 30 for water) before $k$ is computed. The missing-data handling that applies to every other metric therefore extends to the coefficient's own inputs with no special case.

This differs from the published implementation, which substitutes nothing and instead skips the coefficient entirely (treating $k$ as 1) whenever *either* DT50 is missing, even when the other is known. Concretely, for a substance whose soil DT50 is known and short but whose water DT50 is absent, the approach here still lets that short, real soil DT50 pull $k_{human}$ down through the average, where the published implementation applies no attenuation at all. Using real data where it exists, and the standard precautionary substitution where it does not, is more consistent with how every other missing metric is handled than discarding the known value would be.

## Data quality traceability

The design follows the dissertation's appendix E.2.2 and its proposals on the treatment of missing and unbounded data: keep bounded (`<`, `>`) PPDB values as they are but flag that they are bounded; document worst-case substitutions explicitly rather than only counting them; and go beyond a missing/not-missing binary by recovering the PPDB's own quality band, degraded by one level whenever a value was replaced rather than measured.

**Summary:** three fields per metric, independent of one another:

- `status` — `"measured"` (the metric's primary PPDB field), `"completed"` (derived from a secondary field or a formula: soil DT50 lab or typical, KFOC from KOC, the BCF and GUS formulas, the “stable in water” reading), `"substituted_precautionary"` or `"substituted_null_hazard"` (absent, filled in at scoring time), or `"missing"` (reachable only under `"complete_only"`, where nothing is substituted). Two further values, `"imputed_related_substance"` and `"imputed_family_mean"`, are reserved for the extensions of section [Planned extensions](#planned-extensions) and produced by no code path today; they are named now so that the schema will not need revisiting once they exist.
- `bound` — `"exact"`, `"<"`, `">"`, `"range"` (a resolved text range), or `"missing"` wherever no bound applies at all, which covers a genuinely blank field, a categorical metric with no comparator concept, and a formula-derived value with no comparator to inherit. One label covers all three: distinguishing why no bound applies was judged not worth a second vocabulary for this purpose. The comparator is read from the PPDB's own dedicated column where the export has one, and otherwise from a leading `<` or `>` in the value cell (the database's own statement being more reliable than text-sniffing every cell).
- `confidence` — the PPDB's 1–5 quality band, one level lower whenever the value was completed rather than measured. Undefined for a substituted or imputed value, which has no band to inherit because it was never measured.

**Output.** A `Data_quality` sheet carries one row per substance and metric. The main results sheet instead carries seven compact per-substance counts, so that it can be filtered and sorted without opening the detail. The value itself is not repeated in the detail sheet: it is already in the results, and this sheet is about provenance. By default the sheet covers only the substances that were actually scored (an excluded substance already has its reason summarised in the results).

**Contribution shares.** Counting how many metrics were substituted says little about how much the score rests on them: a substituted metric with a small weight and a low score contributes almost nothing, while a heavily weighted one can dominate. The results sheet therefore reports the percentage of the HPLI (overall and per compartment) attributable to substituted values, and separately to completed ones. The two are kept apart rather than merged: a completed value is real PPDB-derived data, qualitatively different from a worst-case fill-in. Each metric's contribution is its weighted score after the persistence coefficient, so the shares are built from the same quantities the HPLI itself is.

## Weight calculation

Within each compartment: a Spearman correlation matrix over that compartment's metrics, each metric's weight proportional to 1/(sum of its absolute correlations with its peers), normalised to sum to 1 within the compartment, then rescaled to the compartment's fixed share (a third each for environmental fate and human toxicity, a sixth each for terrestrial and aquatic ecotoxicity). A metric strongly correlated with its peers is largely already represented by them and earns a lower weight; one bringing unique information earns a higher one.

**The correlations run over every substance of the PPDB export**, not only over the active substances the indicator reports on. Banned substances, metabolites and co-formulants carry physico-chemical and (eco)toxicological properties just as authorised active substances do, and including them ties the weights to the intrinsic relationships between metrics rather than to the regulatory status a substance happens to have at a given date ([Vandevoorde et al., 2025](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1748-9326/ae269b)). A weight computed on the authorised substances only would move with every authorisation or withdrawal, although nothing about the metrics themselves would have changed. The substance scope therefore plays no part in the weights: it enters the indicator through the choice of metrics (section [Adapting the indicator to another metric set](#adapting-the-indicator-to-another-metric-set)) and through the coverage threshold a substance must clear to be scored.

The correlation is computed on **observed data only**. Metric extraction reuses the same code the scoring uses, so a metric is derived identically whether it will be scored or correlated, but without the precautionary substitution: missing values stay missing and each pair is correlated over the substances where both metrics have a value. A weight should reflect an observed relationship, not one flattened or inflated by synthetic worst-case fill-ins. Categorical metrics are numeric-coded by the same 0/0.5/1 mapping the indicator scores them with, rather than one invented for the correlation, so a metric's correlation structure sits on the scale of the score it will eventually weight.

**Two errors in the implementation behind the published weights** are corrected here, and anyone comparing this implementation against the paper will find both.

- *A correlation matrix built on the wrong metric list.* Each compartment's matrix was computed over the full 27-metric Walloon superset, and the 7 metrics outside the EU version were then dropped from the final table without recomputing — so metrics HPLI-EU never scores still shaped every weight it kept. Here each version computes its weights from its own metric list. The effect tracks how many metrics a compartment loses: terrestrial ecotoxicity, losing 5 of 9, moves most (honeybees acute, 7.5% published against 4.6% here), while human toxicity, losing only genotoxicity, barely moves.
- *A fill-in applied upstream of the weight calculation.* A water DT50 recorded only as the free text “stable” is read as 300 days. That reading belongs to metric extraction, which scoring and weighting share, so the weight calculation received it among the real values with no way to tell them apart. Scoring needs a value per substance; a correlation does not, and one constant repeated across every substance carrying the same free-text note manufactures agreement between water DT50 and whatever else they have in common. Extraction now takes the assumption as a parameter and the weight calculation turns it off, while the completions that read another real PPDB field, or apply a published formula over one, are kept: what is dropped is the single fill-in that carries no per-substance information. The effect is small and confined to environmental fate (12 substances of 2711, no weight moving by more than 0.19 percentage points), and its direction confirms the reasoning — withdrawing the fill-ins *lowers* water DT50's own weight, which is what a constant repeated across unrelated substances would be expected to do.

**Output and traceability.** The weights are written with a run log recording when they were computed, from which PPDB export and under which range policy, over how many substances, and for which metric list. The scoring script can load them in place of the hardcoded defaults, but only after checking that the file's metric list matches the active indicator definition exactly (a weights file computed for a different version is refused with a clear error rather than silently mis-applied) and it carries the weights' timestamp into its own run log, so every scored output records which weights produced it.

## Validation

The implementation has been checked against the published appendix of [Vandevoorde et al. (2025)](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1748-9326/ae269b). Environmental fate, terrestrial ecotoxicity and human toxicity match the published values closely. Aquatic ecotoxicity differs on substances with a well-defined water DT50, for the reason given in section [Where the persistence coefficient is applied](#where-the-persistence-coefficient-is-applied).

On the full 3 May 2024 export, 2711 substances are read and **890 pass the 60% coverage threshold** and are scored. The remaining 1821 are reported with their coverage and the list of metrics they lack, rather than silently dropped: the results sheet carries each excluded substance's coverage and its missing metrics, which is why the traceability sheet of section [Data quality traceability](#data-quality-traceability) covers only the substances actually scored.

The weight calculation has been checked the same way: re-enabling the “stable in water” assumption reproduces the earlier weights exactly, all 20 identical to the third decimal of a percentage point, which establishes that the difference documented in section [Weight calculation](#weight-calculation) is attributable to that one choice and to nothing else.

## Rose plot visualisation

A per-substance rose diagram of the scores: one wedge per metric, its angular width proportional to the metric's aggregation weight and its radial height to the normalised hazard score, with the data-quality traceability of section [Data quality traceability](#data-quality-traceability) drawn over it (substituted metrics hatched unconditionally, bound and confidence shown as optional annotations). Completed values are deliberately *not* hatched: they are real PPDB-derived data, and merging them visually with a worst-case fill-in would undo the distinction section [Data quality traceability](#data-quality-traceability) exists to draw. They are instead marked, when the finer detail is asked for, by italicising their confidence label.

**A wedge's angular width is the metric's aggregation weight.** That is the point of the diagram: the full turn is the indicator, and each metric occupies exactly the share of it that the aggregation gives it, so a wedge's visual area reads as its actual contribution to the score rather than as one of equal slices. Wedge positions are consequently the cumulative weight, in the order the parameters sheet lists metrics (compartment by compartment) and the radial dividers between compartments fall out of the same cumulative sum rather than from a second, hardcoded copy of the compartment shares. On the published HPLI-EU definition they land at 0, 1/3, 1/2 and 2/3.

**It reads a saved results workbook and nothing else** (not live PPDB data, and not in-memory objects from the scoring script, which it does not source). That workbook is the full one kept locally, since the publishable one carries no per-metric score (section [PPDB licensing and what may be shared](#ppdb-licensing-and-what-may-be-shared)). Every quantity the plot needs, the metric list and the weights included, comes from that workbook's own parameters sheet. The consequence is deliberate: the wedge widths always match the weights that actually produced the run being plotted, rather than whatever the indicator definition happens to contain now. A plot is therefore a faithful picture of one specific scored dataset, and re-scoring cannot silently change the meaning of a figure already drawn.

Everything else there is presentation rather than method: human-readable metric labels, and one colour gradient per compartment sized to however many metrics that compartment holds, so that adding or removing a metric needs no palette upkeep.

## PPDB licensing and what may be shared

**The PPDB export is not redistributable.** The database is freely browsable online, but the offline export this code reads is a licensed AERU product. It is never committed to the repository, and anyone wanting to run these scripts needs their own access to the PPDB from the University of Hertfordshire. The export folder is therefore left unset in the published scripts, to be filled in locally, and the run logs identify the export by its folder name and the checksums of its four workbooks rather than by where it is stored.

**Per-metric scores are not at a safe distance from the source data.** Each normalisation is piecewise linear and monotone between thresholds that are themselves published (in *Table 1* and in the parameters sheet of every results workbook), so a metric score converts back into the raw value it was computed from. The conversion is exact except where the score is floored at 0, which only says that the raw value lies beyond the null-hazard threshold, and, for the chronic metrics, where the persistence coefficient has been applied and the DT50 behind it is not known. A table of per-metric scores for every substance of an export therefore amounts to a re-encoded copy of a substantial part of the database, which is the kind of derived dataset the AERU terms of use do not allow without written permission.

**What is published is the aggregated output.** Each run of `HPLI score.R` writes two workbooks. The publishable one, `HPLI_results.xlsx`, holds for every substance the HPLI and its four compartment scores, the data-quality indicators (each metric's provenance, bound and confidence band, the per-substance counts derived from them, and the share of each score resting on values that were not measured), the indicator definition the run used, and the run log. It is the one meant to be shared, as a reference against which a run on another export, or a reimplementation, can be compared; this version of the repository does not include one, and a reference output is to be published with a later version. The full workbook adds every per-metric score (and, with `export_raw_ppdb_values = TRUE`, the raw and completed values themselves). It is what the rose diagrams of section [Rose plot visualisation](#rose-plot-visualisation) are drawn from, and should stay local.

The weights workbook holds one weight per metric and no per-substance value, so it cannot be read back into the database. The inert-substance reference file records PPDB substance IDs, a review status and hand-written comments, which quote a few PPDB values where they support a decision, rather than the PPDB property fields themselves.

**Every output workbook states its own terms.** Workbooks circulate on their own, away from the repository, so each one opens on a notice sheet: it is derived from the PPDB; it is covered neither by the licence of the code nor by that of this document; it is subject to the AERU terms and conditions of use; and it says how to cite both the PPDB and the HPLI.

## Adapting the indicator to another metric set

**Why there is more than one metric set.** The indicator's metric list is not fixed a priori. A metric enters it when at least 60% of the substances in scope carry data for it (section [Missing-data and range-value policy](#missing-data-and-range-value-policy)), so the scope decides the list. HPLI-EU's 20 metrics are those clearing that bar across all active substances authorised in the European Union in May 2024 (a broad population, and an unevenly documented one, since many substances are authorised for narrow uses and carry sparse ecotoxicological data).

An indicator meant for one country or region is interested in a much smaller set: the substances actually used there. That set is better documented on average, so more metrics clear the same 60% bar. The Walloon HPLI-Wal version is built this way, on the list of active substances used in Wallonia, and reaches 27 metrics where the European scope supports 20. The extra seven are not a methodological addition — they are the same rule applied to a scope in which more data exists.

A version adapted to another national reporting context would be derived the same way, from that country's own list of substances in use. In the current version of the code, the metric selection is an *input* to this implementation, not something it performs. The metric list arrives already decided, in the definition file (and re-deriving it is listed as a planned extension; cf. section [Planned extensions](#planned-extensions)).

**A local HPLI version must compute its own weights**, and cannot borrow another's. The inverse-correlation weighting is defined *within* a compartment and over the metrics that compartment contains, so adding or removing a metric changes every other weight in that compartment (not only the new one's). This is the substance of the divergence documented in section [Weight calculation](#weight-calculation), and it is a property of the method rather than an implementation detail. The weight calculation follows the active definition automatically, and the scoring script refuses a weights file whose metric list does not match, so the mistake is caught rather than silently made.

**A methodological question a larger metric set reopens:** whether the persistence coefficient applies to a chronic metric whose mechanism is not persistence-related: $k_{soil}$ has no independent use in the 20-metric set, but a soil-based chronic ecotoxicity metric (the earthworm chronic NOEC, for instance) gives it one, and the per-pathway reasoning of section [Completing soil DT50, KFOC, BCF and GUS before normalisation](#completing-soil-dt50-kfoc-bcf-and-gus-before-normalisation) would have to be redone for it rather than inherited.

## Planned extensions

Not yet implemented, planned for 2027:

1. Deriving the metric selection itself, rather than taking it as given (section [Adapting the indicator to another metric set](#adapting-the-indicator-to-another-metric-set)), with the metric-side 60% bar exposed as its own setting alongside `coverage_threshold`. Which metrics clear that bar depends on the substance scope and on the data available at the time, and both move: authorisations change, and the PPDB fills in. This applies to the EU metric set as much as to any local adaptation, and neither is re-derived today. Selecting a local substance set — the 27-metric HPLI-Wal version, for instance — then becomes an application of the same computation rather than a second metric list maintained separately.
1. Missing-data imputation by pesticide functional group (UK-PLI style, [Tzilivakis et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1080/03601234.2025.2610127): the arithmetic mean over active substances of the same pesticide type; see also dissertation appendix E.2.2) as an alternative to the current worst-case substitution, including for a missing soil or water DT50 feeding the persistence coefficient and not only for their own scores. Reserved in the traceability schema as `imputed_family_mean`.
1. Missing-data substitution from a chemically related substance (cf. the UK-PLI's first substitution step, before the completion hierarchy and before the type-mean fallback above; see dissertation appendix E.2.2, e.g. chlormequat chloride's values used for chlormequat). Reserved in the schema as `imputed_related_substance`.
1. A configurable persistence-coefficient pair, exposing the choice between the published 180/7 and the dissertation's 230/33 (section [Where the persistence coefficient is applied](#where-the-persistence-coefficient-is-applied)).
1. Extending the hand-verified inert-substance list of section [Completing soil DT50, KFOC, BCF and GUS before normalisation](#completing-soil-dt50-kfoc-bcf-and-gus-before-normalisation) as more substances are encountered.

These would make the implementation general-purpose and transferable across national datasets, rather than tied to the EU metric set and the PPDB defaults it currently hardcodes.
